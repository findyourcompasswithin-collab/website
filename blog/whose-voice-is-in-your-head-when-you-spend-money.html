Whose voice is in your head when you spend money?

Whose voice do you hear when you spend money, and is it actually yours?

This article addresses the psychological relationship with money. It is not financial advice. For investment, tax, or financial planning guidance, please consult a qualified financial professional.

Most financial advice starts with behaviour: spend less, save more, invest early, track your expenses. This advice is not wrong. It is also, for a large proportion of people, largely ineffective. Not because the behaviour is complicated, but because the behaviour is not the problem.

A person can know exactly what they should be doing with money and still not do it. They can build a budget and ignore it. They can commit to saving and spend the money anyway. They can understand compound interest and still feel unable to start. And the reason is not a lack of information. It is a story, running underneath the information, that is driving the behaviour instead.

Where money beliefs come from

Financial psychologist Dr Brad Klontz has spent years researching what he calls "money scripts": the core beliefs about money that drive financial behaviour. His research consistently finds that these scripts are formed in childhood, typically before the age of twelve, by observing how the adults around us related to money.

These observations become beliefs. Money is dangerous. Money means security. People with money are not trustworthy. Asking for more money is greedy. There is never enough. You have to work yourself to exhaustion to deserve what you have. These beliefs are not examined. They are absorbed. And they operate below the level of conscious decision-making, shaping financial behaviour in ways that more information alone cannot touch.

Until the story changes, the spreadsheet does not. Behaviour follows belief, not the other way around.

The four money scripts

Klontz identifies four primary money scripts, each with its own pattern of financial behaviour:

  • Money avoidance: the belief that money is bad, corrupting, or that you do not deserve it. Often produces chronic underpaying yourself, giving money away, or self-sabotaging financial progress.
  • Money worship: the belief that more money will solve your problems and that you will be happy when you have enough. Often produces endless striving, workaholism, and a moving goalpost for what enough means.
  • Money status: the belief that your net worth equals your self-worth. Often produces overspending to appear successful, hiding debt, and a brittle self-image tied to financial performance.
  • Money vigilance: the belief that you must always be cautious, saving, and watchful because financial security can disappear at any moment. Often produces anxiety about spending even when there is enough, and difficulty enjoying what you have.

Most people carry more than one of these scripts simultaneously, often in direct conflict with each other. And most people have never named them.

Why naming them matters

A belief that has never been named is a belief that cannot be examined. It just runs. It shapes what you charge for your work, what you ask for in salary negotiations, what you allow yourself to spend on your own health, what you believe about whether you deserve financial security, and how you respond when money comes in.

The Money Mindset workbook is built around exactly this process: naming the scripts, tracing where they came from, examining whether they are still serving you, and beginning to build a relationship with money that is actually yours rather than inherited.

What the practical work looks like

The workbook works through several stages. The first is identifying your current money story, the beliefs and emotions that show up when you think about money, spend it, earn it, or talk about it. The second is tracing those beliefs to their source, not to assign blame, but to understand that these were absorbed rather than chosen. The third is examining each belief: is it still true? Was it ever fully true? What evidence exists for and against it? The fourth is building a new relationship with money, one grounded in your current values and actual circumstances rather than the household you grew up in.

The workbook also includes practical sections on financial clarity: understanding what you have, what you owe, what you need, and what you want. Not because the practical work is all there is, but because clarity about the numbers is hard to build when the emotional relationship underneath is unexamined.

The voice in your head

When you spend money on something for yourself and feel guilty, whose voice is that? When you undercharge for your work and feel unable to ask for more, whose belief is that? When you cannot bring yourself to look at your bank account, what story is running that makes looking feel dangerous?

These voices are not random. They came from somewhere. And most of the time, they came from someone who was doing the best they could with what they knew, in circumstances that are not yours. You have inherited the script. You do not have to keep running it.

The money work starts not with a spreadsheet, but with a question: what do I actually believe about money, and where did that belief come from?
